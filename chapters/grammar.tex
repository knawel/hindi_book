\subsection{1.2 Nouns \& Gender}

Hindi has two grammatical genders: \textbf{masculine} and \textbf{feminine}. Gender affects adjective and verb agreement throughout the sentence.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Most masculine nouns end in \textbf{-aa}: \textit{ladkaa} (\hw{लड़का}) — boy
  \item Most feminine nouns end in \textbf{-ii}: \textit{ladkii} (\hw{लड़की}) — girl
  \item Many exceptions exist — always learn gender with the noun.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Plurals}

\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}

  \midrule

  Masc.\ (other endings) & — & unchanged & \textit{ghar} → \textit{ghar} \\

\subsection{1.6 Verbs (\hw{क्रिया})}

\subsubsection{Infinitive}
All Hindi infinitives end in \textbf{-naa}: e.g.\ \textit{jaanaa} (\hw{जाना}) — \textit{to go}.
Root = infinitive minus \textit{-naa}: \textit{jaa-}.

\subsubsection{The verb \textit{honaa} (\hw{होना}) — to be}

\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
  \toprule
  Subject & Present & Past (m. / f.) & Hindi (present; past) \\
  \midrule
  main        & hoon  & thaa / thii   & \hw{हूँ; था / थी} \\
  tuu         & hai   & thaa / thii   & \hw{है; था / थी} \\
  tum         & ho    & the / thiin   & \hw{हो; थे / थीं} \\
  yah/vah     & hai   & thaa / thii   & \hw{है; था / थी} \\
  aap/ham/ve  & hain  & the / thiin   & \hw{हैं; थे / थीं} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsubsection{Present Habitual Tense}

\pattern{Subject + [Object] + verb-stem + taa/tii/te + hoon/hai/hain/ho}

\textit{-taa} → masculine singular \quad \textit{-tii} → feminine \quad \textit{-te} → masculine plural / formal

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  main chaay piitaa hoon & \hw{मैं चाय पीता हूँ।} & I drink tea. (m.) \\
  vah roz jaatii hai      & \hw{वह रोज़ जाती है।}  & She goes every day. \\
  ham Hindi siikhte hain   & \hw{हम हिंदी सीखते हैं।} & We learn Hindi. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsubsection{Present Continuous Tense}

\pattern{Subject + verb-stem + rahaa/rahii/rahe + hoon/hai/hain/ho}

\textit{-rahaa} → masculine singular \quad \textit{-rahii} → feminine \quad \textit{-rahe} → plural / formal

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  main Hindi siikh rahaa hoon     & \hw{मैं हिंदी सीख रहा हूँ।}       & I am learning Hindi. (m.) \\
  merii patnii khaa rahii hai    & \hw{मेरी पत्नी खाना खा रही है।}   & My wife is eating. \\
  aap kyaa kar rahe ho?          & \hw{आप क्या कर रहे हो?}            & What are you doing? \\
  bacchaa ananaas khaa rahaa hai & \hw{बच्चा अनानास खा रहा है।}       & The child is eating pineapple. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsubsection{Simple Past Tense (\hw{भूतकाल})}

\pattern{Intransitive: Subject + verb-stem + aa/ii/e + (thaa/thii/the)}
\pattern{Transitive:\ \ \ \ Subject\,+\,\textbf{ne} + Object + verb-pp + thaa/thii/the}

Transitive verbs (those taking a direct object) require \textbf{-ne} on the subject.\\
Intransitive verbs (motion, states) do \emph{not} take \textit{-ne}.

\noindent\textit{Note:} plain \hw{मैं गया} = I went. Adding \hw{था} (\hw{मैं गया था}) = I had gone / I went (some time ago). In conversation both are heard; for ``yesterday I did X'' the plain form is the default.

\textbf{Irregular past participles} (learn these by heart):

  \midrule
  jaanaa & gayaa  & \hw{जाना} → \hw{गया}   & went \\
  karnaa & kiyaa  & \hw{करना} → \hw{किया}  & did \\
  denaa  & diyaa  & \hw{देना} → \hw{दिया}  & gave \\
  lenaa  & liyaa  & \hw{लेना} → \hw{लिया}  & took \\
  piinaa & piyaa  & \hw{पीना} → \hw{पिया}  & drank \\
  honaa  & huaa   & \hw{होना} → \hw{हुआ}   & happened / became \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule

\subsubsection{Present Perfect — \textit{have done}}

\pattern{Subject\,+\,\textbf{ne} + Object + verb-pp + hai/hain \ \ \textnormal{(transitive)}}
\pattern{Subject + verb-pp + hoon/hai/hain \ \ \textnormal{(intransitive)}}

Same as the simple past, but ends in \textit{hai/hain} instead of \textit{thaa/the}. Used for experiences and results that still matter now. With \textit{ne}, the verb agrees with the \emph{object} (see 1.11).

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  mainne kuTTuu khaayaa hai            & \hw{मैंने कुट्टू खाया है।}          & I have eaten buckwheat. \\
    mainne kuTTuu ke phuul dekhe hain    & \hw{मैंने कुट्टू के फूल देखे हैं।}   & I've seen buckwheat flowers. \\
  main Ladakh gayaa hoon               & \hw{मैं लद्दाख गया हूँ।}             & I've been to Ladakh. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsubsection{Imperative — commands \& requests}

Three levels of politeness, matching the three words for "you". Use the \textbf{aap} form by default (teacher, strangers, shops).

\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
  \toprule
  Level & Formation & Example & Hindi \\
  \midrule
  \textit{tuu} (intimate/rude) & stem       & jaa, khaa, baiTh     & \hw{जा, खा, बैठ} \\
  \textit{tum} (familiar)      & stem + o   & jaao, khaao, baiTho  & \hw{जाओ, खाओ, बैठो} \\
  \textit{aap} (polite)        & stem + iye & jaaiye, khaaiye, baiThiye & \hw{जाइए, खाइए, बैठिए} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\pattern{Subject \ \ [Indirect Object + ko] \ \ [Direct Object] \ \ [Adverb] \ \ Verb}

\subsection{1.9 Obligation — \textit{ko + Infinitive + honaa}}

Used to express necessity (``must'', ``have to''). The subject takes oblique form + \textit{ko}; the infinitive stays unchanged.

\pattern{Subject (oblique + ko) + Infinitive + hogaa / hai}

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  mujhe jaanaa hogaa       & \hw{मुझे जाना होगा।}         & I will have to go. \\
  aapko Nepaal aanaa hogaa & \hw{आपको नेपाल आना होगा।}    & You must come to Nepal. \\
  mujhe 5 kilomiiTar dauRnaa hogaa & \hw{मुझे 5 किलोमीटर दौड़ना होगा।}  & I will have to run 5 km. \\
  tumhen kaam karnaa hai   & \hw{तुम्हें काम करना है।}     & You have to work. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule

  \midrule

\subsection{1.11 Ergative Agreement — verb agrees with the object}

With \textit{ne} (transitive verbs in past/perfect), the verb does \textbf{not} agree with the subject. It agrees in gender and number with the \textbf{object}.

\pattern{Subject\,+\,ne + Object (m./f., sg./pl.) + verb-pp matching the object}

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  mainne saaikal chalaaii        & \hw{मैंने साइकिल चलाई।}       & I rode a bike. (f.) \\
  mainne kuTTuu khaayaa          & \hw{मैंने कुट्टू खाया।}        & I ate buckwheat. (m.sg.) \\
  mainne phuul dekhe             & \hw{मैंने फूल देखे।}           & I saw flowers. (m.pl.) \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent\textit{Common mistake:} \hw{मैं साइकिल चलाई} (wrong) → \hw{मैंने साइकिल चलाई} (right). \textit{chalaanaa} is transitive, so the subject needs \textit{ne}.

\subsection{1.12 Oblique Case of Nouns}

Before \textbf{any postposition} (\textit{ko, se, men, par, kaa/kii/ke, tak, ke saath}\ldots), a noun changes to its \textbf{oblique} form. Only masculine \textit{-aa} nouns change in the singular; \textbf{all} nouns take \textit{-on} in the oblique plural.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lllll@{}}
  \toprule
  Type & Sg.\ direct & Sg.\ oblique & Pl.\ direct & Pl.\ oblique \\
  \midrule
  m.\ \textit{-aa}   & \hw{लड़का} laRkaa & \hw{लड़के} laRke  & \hw{लड़के} laRke       & \hw{लड़कों} laRkon \\
  m.\ other          & \hw{घर} ghar      & \hw{घर} ghar      & \hw{घर} ghar           & \hw{घरों} gharon \\
  f.\ \textit{-ii}   & \hw{लड़की} laRkii & \hw{लड़की} laRkii & \hw{लड़कियाँ} laRkiyaan & \hw{लड़कियों} laRkiyon \\
  f.\ other          & \hw{किताब} kitaab & \hw{किताब} kitaab & \hw{किताबें} kitaaben  & \hw{किताबों} kitaabon \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\textit{-aa} adjectives go oblique too: \textit{baRaa kamraa} → \textit{baRe kamre men}.
Exceptions: kinship words like \textit{pitaa}, \textit{chaachaa} do not change in the singular (\hw{पिता को}).

\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.0cm}p{4.8cm}@{}}

\subsection{1.13 Possessives \& \textit{apnaa}}

Possessives behave like \textit{-aa} adjectives: \textit{-aa / -e / -ii} agree with the \textbf{possessed} thing, not the owner.

  \toprule
  Owner & m.sg. / m.pl. / f. & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  main   & meraa / mere / merii             & \hw{मेरा / मेरे / मेरी}         & my \\
  tuu    & teraa / tere / terii             & \hw{तेरा / तेरे / तेरी}         & your (intimate) \\
  tum    & tumhaaraa / -e / -ii             & \hw{तुम्हारा / तुम्हारे / तुम्हारी} & your (familiar) \\
  aap    & aapkaa / -ke / -kii              & \hw{आपका / आपके / आपकी}       & your (polite) \\
  yah    & iskaa / -ke / -kii               & \hw{इसका / इसके / इसकी}       & his/her/its (near) \\
  vah    & uskaa / -ke / -kii               & \hw{उसका / उसके / उसकी}       & his/her/its (far) \\
  ham    & hamaaraa / -e / -ii              & \hw{हमारा / हमारे / हमारी}     & our \\

\subsubsection{\textit{apnaa} (\hw{अपना}) — one's own}

When the owner is the \textbf{subject of the same sentence}, use \textit{apnaa/apne/apnii} instead of \textit{meraa, uskaa}\ldots

\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.0cm}p{4.8cm}@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  main apne ghar jaataa hoon     & \hw{मैं अपने घर जाता हूँ।}      & I go to my (own) home. \\
  vah apnii kitaab paRhtii hai   & \hw{वह अपनी किताब पढ़ती है।}    & She reads her own book. \\
  vah uskii kitaab paRhtii hai   & \hw{वह उसकी किताब पढ़ती है।}    & She reads her (someone else's) book. \\
  meraa ghar baRaa hai           & \hw{मेरा घर बड़ा है।}            & My house is big. (\textit{ghar} is the subject → \textit{meraa}) \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent\textit{Common mistake:} \hw{मैं मेरे घर जाता हूँ} (wrong) → \hw{मैं अपने घर जाता हूँ} (right).

\subsection{1.14 Conditionals — \textit{agar \ldots{} to}}

\pattern{agar + [condition] , to + [result]}

\textit{agar} (\hw{अगर}) = if; \textit{to} (\hw{तो}) = then. \textit{to} is almost never dropped. For real future conditions, both halves usually use the future tense (English uses present after "if").

\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.0cm}p{4.8cm}@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  agar baarish hogii, to main ghar par rahuungaa & \hw{अगर बारिश होगी, तो मैं घर पर रहूँगा।} & If it rains, I'll stay at home. \\
  agar tum aaoge, to ham saath khaaenge & \hw{अगर तुम आओगे, तो हम साथ खाएँगे।} & If you come, we'll eat together. \\
  agar aap thake hain, to aaraam kiijiye & \hw{अगर आप थके हैं, तो आराम कीजिए।} & If you are tired, rest. \\
  agar mujhe samay milegaa, to main Nepaal jaaungaa & \hw{अगर मुझे समय मिलेगा, तो मैं नेपाल जाऊँगा।} & If I get time, I'll go to Nepal. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent\textit{Later (B1):} unreal conditions use the habitual form without \textit{thaa}: \hw{अगर मैं अमीर होता, तो\ldots} = If I were rich, \ldots
