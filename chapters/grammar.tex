  Retroflex & \hw{ट} Ta & \hw{ठ} Tha & \hw{ड} Da & \hw{ढ} Dha & \hw{ण} Na \\
  Dental & \hw{त} ta & \hw{थ} tha & \hw{द} da & \hw{ध} dha & \hw{न} na \\

\textbf{Others:} \hw{य} ya, \hw{र} ra, \hw{ल} la, \hw{व} va, \hw{श} sha, \hw{ष} Sha, \hw{स} sa, \hw{ह} ha.\\
\textbf{With a dot (nukta):} \hw{ड़} Ra, \hw{ढ़} Rha, \hw{ज़} za, \hw{फ़} fa, \hw{क़} qa, \hw{ख़} kha. \hw{ड़} (\textit{laRkaa}, \textit{pahaaR}) is very common.\\

\subsubsection{Vowel Signs (Maatraa — \hw{मात्रा})}

After a consonant, a vowel is written as a \textbf{sign} attached to it, not as the full letter. The full letters (above) are used only at the start of a word or after another vowel: \hw{आम} \textit{aam}, but \hw{काम} \textit{kaam}.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lllll@{}}
  \toprule
  Vowel & Sign & With \hw{क} & Reads & Example word \\
  \midrule
  a   & (none) & \hw{क}  & ka   & \hw{कम} \textit{kam} (less) \\
  aa  & \hw{ा} & \hw{का} & kaa  & \hw{काम} \textit{kaam} (work) \\
  i   & \hw{ि} & \hw{कि} & ki   & \hw{किताब} \textit{kitaab} (book) \\
  ii  & \hw{ी} & \hw{की} & kii  & \hw{चीनी} \textit{chiinii} (sugar) \\
  u   & \hw{ु} & \hw{कु} & ku   & \hw{कुत्ता} \textit{kuttaa} (dog) \\
  uu  & \hw{ू} & \hw{कू} & kuu  & \hw{फूल} \textit{phuul} (flower) \\
  ri  & \hw{ृ} & \hw{कृ} & kri  & \hw{कृपया} \textit{kripayaa} (please) \\
  e   & \hw{े} & \hw{के} & ke   & \hw{सेब} \textit{seb} (apple) \\
  ai  & \hw{ै} & \hw{कै} & kai  & \hw{पैसा} \textit{paisaa} (money) \\
  o   & \hw{ो} & \hw{को} & ko   & \hw{दोस्त} \textit{dost} (friend) \\
  au  & \hw{ौ} & \hw{कौ} & kau  & \hw{कौन} \textit{kaun} (who) \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\textbf{Reading traps:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \hw{ि} (\textit{i}) is \emph{written before} the consonant but \emph{said after} it: \hw{कि} = \textit{ki}.
  \item \textbf{Nasal dot} \hw{ं} and \textbf{moon-dot} \hw{ँ} nasalise the vowel: \hw{हैं} \textit{hain}, \hw{माँ} \textit{maan}. Before a consonant \hw{ं} sounds like \textit{n/m}: \hw{हिंदी} \textit{hindii}.
  \item \textbf{Halant} \hw{्} removes the built-in \textit{a}: \hw{क्} = \textit{k}. Usually two consonants just join: \hw{स्कूल} \textit{skuul}, \hw{दोस्त} \textit{dost}.
  \item \hw{र} changes shape in clusters: \hw{प्र} \textit{pra} (below), \hw{र्क} \textit{rka} (hook above).
  \item The final \textit{a} of a word is silent: \hw{काम} is \textit{kaam}, not \textit{kaama}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{1.2 Nouns \& Gender}

Hindi has two grammatical genders: \textbf{masculine} and \textbf{feminine}. Gender affects adjective and verb agreement throughout the sentence.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Most masculine nouns end in \textbf{-aa}: \textit{laRkaa} (\hw{लड़का}) — boy
  \item Most feminine nouns end in \textbf{-ii}: \textit{laRkii} (\hw{लड़की}) — girl
  \item Many exceptions exist — always learn gender with the noun.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{1.6 Verbs (\hw{क्रिया})}

\subsubsection{Infinitive}
All Hindi infinitives end in \textbf{-naa}: e.g.\ \textit{jaanaa} (\hw{जाना}) — \textit{to go}.
Root = infinitive minus \textit{-naa}: \textit{jaa-}.

\subsubsection{The verb \textit{honaa} (\hw{होना}) — to be}

  \toprule

  \midrule
  main        & huun  & thaa / thii   & \hw{हूँ; था / थी} \\
  tuu         & hai   & thaa / thii   & \hw{है; था / थी} \\
  tum         & ho    & the / thiin   & \hw{हो; थे / थीं} \\
  yah/vah     & hai   & thaa / thii   & \hw{है; था / थी} \\

\subsubsection{Present Habitual Tense}

\pattern{Subject + [Object] + verb-stem + taa/tii/te + huun/hai/hain/ho}

\textit{-taa} → masculine singular \quad \textit{-tii} → feminine \quad \textit{-te} → masculine plural / formal

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  main chaay piitaa huun & \hw{मैं चाय पीता हूँ।} & I drink tea. (m.) \\
  vah roz jaatii hai      & \hw{वह रोज़ जाती है।}  & She goes every day. \\
  ham Hindi siikhte hain   & \hw{हम हिंदी सीखते हैं।} & We learn Hindi. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsubsection{Present Continuous Tense}

\pattern{Subject + verb-stem + rahaa/rahii/rahe + huun/hai/hain/ho}

\textit{-rahaa} → masculine singular \quad \textit{-rahii} → feminine \quad \textit{-rahe} → plural / formal

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  main Hindi siikh rahaa huun     & \hw{मैं हिंदी सीख रहा हूँ।}       & I am learning Hindi. (m.) \\
  merii patnii khaa rahii hai    & \hw{मेरी पत्नी खाना खा रही है।}   & My wife is eating. \\
  aap kyaa kar rahe hain?          & \hw{आप क्या कर रहे हैं?}            & What are you doing? \\
  bachchaa ananaas khaa rahaa hai & \hw{बच्चा अनानास खा रहा है।}       & The child is eating pineapple. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsubsection{Simple Past Tense (\hw{भूतकाल})}

\pattern{Intransitive: Subject + verb-stem + aa/ii/e + (thaa/thii/the)}
\pattern{Transitive:\ \ \ \ Subject\,+\,\textbf{ne} + Object + verb-pp + thaa/thii/the}

Transitive verbs (those taking a direct object) require \textbf{-ne} on the subject.\\
Intransitive verbs (motion, states) do \emph{not} take \textit{-ne}.

\noindent\textit{Note:} plain \hw{मैं गया} = I went. Adding \hw{था} (\hw{मैं गया था}) = I had gone / I went (some time ago). In conversation both are heard; for ``yesterday I did X'' the plain form is the default.

  \midrule

  karnaa & kiyaa  & \hw{करना} → \hw{किया}  & did \\
  denaa  & diyaa  & \hw{देना} → \hw{दिया}  & gave \\
  lenaa  & liyaa  & \hw{लेना} → \hw{लिया}  & took \\

\subsubsection{Present Perfect — \textit{have done}}

\pattern{Subject\,+\,\textbf{ne} + Object + verb-pp + hai/hain \ \ \textnormal{(transitive)}}
\pattern{Subject + verb-pp + huun/hai/hain \ \ \textnormal{(intransitive)}}

Same as the simple past, but ends in \textit{hai/hain} instead of \textit{thaa/the}. Used for experiences and results that still matter now. With \textit{ne}, the verb agrees with the \emph{object} (see 1.11).

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  mainne kuTTuu khaayaa hai            & \hw{मैंने कुट्टू खाया है।}          & I have eaten buckwheat. \\

  main Ladakh gayaa huun               & \hw{मैं लद्दाख गया हूँ।}             & I've been to Ladakh. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

  Language & Order & Example \\

  English & S–V–O & I drink tea. \\
  Hindi   & S–O–V & \hw{मैं चाय पीता हूँ।} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsection{1.9 Obligation — \textit{ko + Infinitive + honaa}}

Used to express necessity (``must'', ``have to''). The subject takes oblique form + \textit{ko}; the infinitive stays unchanged.

\pattern{Subject (oblique + ko) + Infinitive + hogaa / hai}

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  mujhe jaanaa hogaa       & \hw{मुझे जाना होगा।}         & I will have to go. \\
  aapko Nepaal aanaa hogaa & \hw{आपको नेपाल आना होगा।}    & You must come to Nepal. \\
  mujhe 5 kilomiiTar dauRnaa hogaa & \hw{मुझे 5 किलोमीटर दौड़ना होगा।}  & I will have to run 5 km. \\
  tumhen kaam karnaa hai   & \hw{तुम्हें काम करना है।}     & You have to work. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsection{1.10 Comparison (\textit{se} + adjective)}

The reference point (what you compare \emph{against}) takes \textit{se}. The adjective agrees with the subject.

\pattern{X + Y-se + baRaa/chhoTaa (inflected) + hai}

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  merii bahan mujhse baRii hai    & \hw{मेरी बहन मुझसे बड़ी है।}   & My sister is older than me. \\
  meraa bhaaii mujhse chhoTaa hai & \hw{मेरा भाई मुझसे छोटा है।}  & My brother is younger than me. \\
  Ruus ek baRaa desh hai          & \hw{रूस एक बड़ा देश है।}       & Russia is a big country. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsubsection{More / less than — \textit{se zyaadaa / se kam}}

Adjectives without a comparative form (\textit{mahangaa}, \textit{sundar}\ldots) use \textit{zyaadaa} (more) or \textit{kam} (less). The same words work with numbers and with \textit{pasand}.

\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.0cm}p{4.8cm}@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  yah usse zyaadaa mahangaa hai & \hw{यह उससे ज़्यादा महँगा है।}   & This is more expensive than that. \\
  Dillii Mumbaii se kam baRii hai & \hw{दिल्ली मुंबई से कम बड़ी है।} & Delhi is less big than Mumbai. \\
  60 kilomiiTar se zyaadaa      & \hw{60 किलोमीटर से ज़्यादा}      & more than 60 km \\
  mujhe chaay se zyaadaa kaufii pasand hai & \hw{मुझे चाय से ज़्यादा कॉफ़ी पसंद है।} & I like coffee more than tea. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsubsection{Superlative — \textit{sabse} + adjective}

\pattern{sabse + adjective (inflected) \ \ = \ \ the most \ldots{} (lit.\ ``than all'')}

  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\subsection{1.11 Ergative Agreement — verb agrees with the object}

With \textit{ne} (transitive verbs in past/perfect), the verb does \textbf{not} agree with the subject. It agrees in gender and number with the \textbf{object}.

\pattern{Subject\,+\,ne + Object (m./f., sg./pl.) + verb-pp matching the object}

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  mainne saaikal chalaaii        & \hw{मैंने साइकिल चलाई।}       & I rode a bike. (f.) \\
  mainne kuTTuu khaayaa          & \hw{मैंने कुट्टू खाया।}        & I ate buckwheat. (m.sg.) \\
  mainne phuul dekhe             & \hw{मैंने फूल देखे।}           & I saw flowers. (m.pl.) \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent\textit{Common mistake:} \hw{मैं साइकिल चलाई} (wrong) → \hw{मैंने साइकिल चलाई} (right). \textit{chalaanaa} is transitive, so the subject needs \textit{ne}.

\subsection{1.12 Oblique Case of Nouns}

Before \textbf{any postposition} (\textit{ko, se, men, par, kaa/kii/ke, tak, ke saath}\ldots), a noun changes to its \textbf{oblique} form. Only masculine \textit{-aa} nouns change in the singular; \textbf{all} nouns take \textit{-on} in the oblique plural.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lllll@{}}
  \toprule
  Type & Sg.\ direct & Sg.\ oblique & Pl.\ direct & Pl.\ oblique \\
  \midrule
  m.\ \textit{-aa}   & \hw{लड़का} laRkaa & \hw{लड़के} laRke  & \hw{लड़के} laRke       & \hw{लड़कों} laRkon \\
  m.\ other          & \hw{घर} ghar      & \hw{घर} ghar      & \hw{घर} ghar           & \hw{घरों} gharon \\
  f.\ \textit{-ii}   & \hw{लड़की} laRkii & \hw{लड़की} laRkii & \hw{लड़कियाँ} laRkiyaan & \hw{लड़कियों} laRkiyon \\
  f.\ other          & \hw{किताब} kitaab & \hw{किताब} kitaab & \hw{किताबें} kitaaben  & \hw{किताबों} kitaabon \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\textit{-aa} adjectives go oblique too: \textit{baRaa kamraa} → \textit{baRe kamre men}.
Exceptions: kinship words like \textit{pitaa}, \textit{chaachaa} do not change in the singular (\hw{पिता को}).

\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.0cm}p{4.8cm}@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  laRke ko paanii do        & \hw{लड़के को पानी दो।}        & Give the boy water. \\
  vah kamre men hai         & \hw{वह कमरे में है।}           & He is in the room. \\
  main bachchon ke saath thaa & \hw{मैं बच्चों के साथ था।}   & I was with the children. \\
  in dukaanon men           & \hw{इन दुकानों में}             & in these shops \\
  baRe shahar se            & \hw{बड़े शहर से}               & from a big city \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Possessives behave like \textit{-aa} adjectives: \textit{-aa / -e / -ii} agree with the \textbf{possessed} thing, not the owner.

\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
  \toprule
  Owner & m.sg. / m.pl. / f. & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  main   & meraa / mere / merii             & \hw{मेरा / मेरे / मेरी}         & my \\
  tuu    & teraa / tere / terii             & \hw{तेरा / तेरे / तेरी}         & your (intimate) \\

  yah    & iskaa / -ke / -kii               & \hw{इसका / इसके / इसकी}       & his/her/its (near) \\

\subsubsection{\textit{apnaa} (\hw{अपना}) — one's own}

When the owner is the \textbf{subject of the same sentence}, use \textit{apnaa/apne/apnii} instead of \textit{meraa, uskaa}\ldots

\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.0cm}p{4.8cm}@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  main apne ghar jaataa huun     & \hw{मैं अपने घर जाता हूँ।}      & I go to my (own) home. \\
  vah apnii kitaab paRhtii hai   & \hw{वह अपनी किताब पढ़ती है।}    & She reads her own book. \\
  vah uskii kitaab paRhtii hai   & \hw{वह उसकी किताब पढ़ती है।}    & She reads her (someone else's) book. \\
  meraa ghar baRaa hai           & \hw{मेरा घर बड़ा है।}            & My house is big. (\textit{ghar} is the subject → \textit{meraa}) \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent\textit{Common mistake:} \hw{मैं मेरे घर जाता हूँ} (wrong) → \hw{मैं अपने घर जाता हूँ} (right).

\subsection{1.14 Conditionals — \textit{agar \ldots{} to}}

\pattern{agar + [condition] , to + [result]}

\textit{agar} (\hw{अगर}) = if; \textit{to} (\hw{तो}) = then. \textit{to} is almost never dropped. For real future conditions, both halves usually use the future tense (English uses present after ``if'').

\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.0cm}p{4.8cm}@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  agar baarish hogii, to main ghar par rahuungaa & \hw{अगर बारिश होगी, तो मैं घर पर रहूँगा।} & If it rains, I'll stay at home. \\
  agar tum aaoge, to ham saath khaaenge & \hw{अगर तुम आओगे, तो हम साथ खाएँगे।} & If you come, we'll eat together. \\
  agar aap thake hain, to aaraam kiijiye & \hw{अगर आप थके हैं, तो आराम कीजिए।} & If you are tired, rest. \\
  agar mujhe samay milegaa, to main Nepaal jaauungaa & \hw{अगर मुझे समय मिलेगा, तो मैं नेपाल जाऊँगा।} & If I get time, I'll go to Nepal. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent\textit{Later (B1):} unreal conditions use the habitual form without \textit{thaa}: \hw{अगर मैं अमीर होता, तो\ldots} = If I were rich, \ldots

\subsection{1.15 Doing One Thing, Then Another — \textit{-kar}}

\pattern{verb-stem + kar \ \ (= having done / after doing) \ \ + main verb}

Joins two actions by the same person. The \textit{-kar} verb never changes; tense, \textit{ne} and agreement all come from the \textbf{final} verb. Exception: \textit{karnaa} → \textit{karke} (not \textit{karkar}).

\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.0cm}p{4.8cm}@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  main naashtaa karke aafis jaataa huun & \hw{मैं नाश्ता करके ऑफिस जाता हूँ।} & I have breakfast and (then) go to the office. \\
  vah ghar aakar chaay piitii hai & \hw{वह घर आकर चाय पीती है।}        & She comes home and drinks tea. \\
  haath dhokar khaaiye           & \hw{हाथ धोकर खाइए।}               & Wash your hands before eating. \\
  main saaikal chalaakar Agham pahunchaa & \hw{मैं साइकिल चलाकर अघम पहुँचा।} & I reached Agham by bike. \\
  aapse milkar khushii huii      & \hw{आपसे मिलकर ख़ुशी हुई।}        & Nice to meet you (lit.\ having met you, joy happened). \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent Note the last-but-one example: \textit{pahunchnaa} (to reach) is intransitive, so no \textit{ne}, even though \textit{chalaanaa} is transitive.

\subsection{1.16 Subjunctive — ``Shall I \ldots?'', ``Let's \ldots'', ``maybe''}

\pattern{Future form \textbf{minus} -gaa/-gii/-ge}

\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
  \toprule
  Subject & Ending & Example & Hindi \\
  \midrule
  main        & -uun & jaauun  & \hw{मैं जाऊँ} \\
  tuu/yah/vah & -e   & jaae    & \hw{वह जाए} \\
  tum         & -o   & jaao    & \hw{तुम जाओ} \\
  aap/ham/ve  & -en  & jaaen   & \hw{हम जाएँ} \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Uses: suggestions, permission, possibility, wishes. It does not change for gender.

\begin{tabular}{@{}p{4.4cm}p{5.0cm}p{4.8cm}@{}}
  \toprule
  Transliteration & Hindi & English \\
  \midrule
  kyaa main andar aauun?          & \hw{क्या मैं अंदर आऊँ?}         & Shall I come in? / May I come in? \\
  main kyaa karuun?               & \hw{मैं क्या करूँ?}            & What should I do? \\
  chalen!                         & \hw{चलें!}                      & Let's go! (colloq.\ \hw{चलो!}) \\
  ham khaanaa khaaen?             & \hw{हम खाना खाएँ?}             & Shall we eat? \\
  shaayad vah kal aae             & \hw{शायद वह कल आए।}            & Maybe he'll come tomorrow. \\
  main chaahtaa huun ki tum aao   & \hw{मैं चाहता हूँ कि तुम आओ।}   & I want you to come. \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\noindent\textit{Tip:} \textit{main chaahtaa huun ki} + subjunctive is how to say ``I want \emph{someone else} to do something''. For yourself, use the infinitive (3.4).
